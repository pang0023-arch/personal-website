Where did prices rise the most?

2021 2022 2023 2024 2025

2021: Prices begin rising

After the Covid lull in 2020, Singapore's resale HDB market woke up. Nationally, median prices increased 10% but the heat did not spread evenly. Mature estates close to the city centre surged the most - Outram (52%), Toa Payoh (30%) and Geylang (26%).

Much of this was driven by flats similar to The Pinnacle @ Duxton, which made headlines for breaking the million-dollar mark. This was deemed as a value-buy as it would mean owning a place in the city fringe at roughly half the price of a nearby private condo.

2022: The heat spreads outward

Prices rose another 11% nationally. While city-fringe estates like Kallang (47%), Toa Payoh (42%) and Outram (39%) stayed hot, the surge began spreading to towns further out. Woodlands (30%), Sembawang (31%) and Bukit Batok (32%) nearly doubled their gains from 2021.

Why? Buyers priced out of mature estates turned to more affordable towns further from the city, and those who couldn't stomach the long Build-To-Order (BTO) wait times flooded to the resale market.

In September 2022, the government stepped in with cooling measures like the 15-month wait-out period for private property owners looking to buy resale HDB. Despite temporarily removing cash-rich downgraders from the market, prices kept climbing.

2023: Cooling measures effective, but not everywhere

The September 2022 measures seemed to work in city-fringe towns. Toa Payoh's cumulative gain slowed by 6% from the previous year, and Kallang by 18%. Prices were still high, but the rate of increase was finally easing.

Yet, Bukit Batok bucked the trend with a 23% YoY surge.

Why? A wave of flats reached their Minimum Occupation Period (MOP) in 2023, flooding the market with new supply in the West of Singapore. Over 2,700 units hit MOP in Bukit Batok alone , the most of any town that year.

These freshly MOP-ed BTO flats often cost more than older resale flats in the same area due to rising BTO launch prices, sellers command a premium and buyers pay it willingly for the longer remaining lease.

In Bukit Batok, this new supply met with strong demand, pushing prices up even as cooling measures slowed the rest of the market.

2024: Demand returns to city fringe

National median prices grew a modest 6% — a sign that the market was cooling broadly. Bukit Batok, which surged in 2023, saw growth flatline to nearly 0% as the wave of MOP supply was absorbed.

But city-fringe towns bounced back. Toa Payoh jumped 34%, while Kallang and Geylang each rose around 19% from the previous year.

Why the reversal? As prices in outer towns climbed through 2022 and 2023, the price gap with city-fringe estates narrowed making the premium feel worth it again. With nearly 2,200 freshly MOPed units entering the market in Toa Payoh, Kallang and Geylang, this was the supply that buyers had been waiting for.

2025: Record highs, unmoved by cooling measures

In August 2024, the government introduced another round of cooling measures. Yet national median prices still rose 8% in 2025, suggesting that underlying demand remains resilient.

Toa Payoh surged 49% from 2024, and Queenstown rose 21%. The pattern from 2024 continued. City-fringe estates with fresh MOP supply attracted the strongest demand. Alkaff CourtView alone brought 1,258 newly MOPed units to Toa Payoh, while Skyparc @ Dawson added 810 units to Queenstown.

Five years on from 2020, few towns have been spared. But the question remains — have incomes kept up with prices?

How is this calculated?

We assume a couple with dual income, since singles under 35 are not eligible to buy resale HDB. Median income by age band is sourced from MOM data. A couple's combined income is taken as twice the individual age-group median, assuming both partners fall within the same age band. This is used to derive the maximum loan based on a Mortgage Servicing Ratio (MSR) of 30% of gross income. We do not account for housing grants as eligibility varies on a case by case basis.

Max monthly mortgage is capped at 30% of combined gross income (MSR). This is converted to a maximum loan amount using a standard annuity formula over 25 years. Loan parameters reflect successive cooling measures — the stress test rate increased from 3.5% to 4% in September 2022, while the Loan-to-Value (LTV) was tightened in three steps: 90% (pre-2021), 85% (December 2021), 80% (September 2022), and 75% (August 2024), meaning buyers must fund a progressively larger share upfront from cash and CPF. Max purchasing power = max loan ÷ LTV. The remainder must be covered by cash and CPF savings.

Why does it feel more difficult than it looks?

On paper, the numbers look manageable. Nationally, median resale prices rose 50% from 2020 to 2025, while borrowing power grew 25% as incomes rose. But successive cooling measures tightened the LTV from 90% to 75%, meaning buyers must fund a larger share upfront. While the loan required grew by only 25% despite a 50% price increase, the cash and CPF component grew by 275% from 2020 to 2025 — hover over The Hidden Burden Chart below to see the breakdown.

Is a resale HDB still within reach?

For couples aged 30–34, purchasing power has broadly kept pace with rising prices, a resale flat remains technically affordable on paper. But for younger buyers aged 25–29, 2024 marked a turning point where median prices exceeded what they could borrow and fund upfront for the first time since 2020.

Beyond the headline numbers, the real squeeze is the upfront cash and CPF burden up 275% since 2020. Even where affordability technically holds, the savings required to get through the door have grown far faster than incomes. Successive cooling measures, while intended to stabilise prices, have also tightened borrowing limits. This means buyers must bring more to the table even as prices climb.

The resale HDB market remains an aspiration for many young Singaporeans, especially those who simply do not have the luxury to wait for 3-5 years for a BTO flat. But the window is narrowing, and the path to ownership demands more than it did just five years ago.

While we have calculated affordability in a monetary sense, this analysis only tells part of the story. Singles under 35 are ineligible to purchase resale HDB flats, leaving an entire group of young Singaporeans without an equivalent pathway to home ownership. The system as it stands ties access to public housing to marital status, a structural constraint that no amount of income growth or cooling measures can address.
